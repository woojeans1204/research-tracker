## On Training Large Language Models for Long-Horizon Tasks: An Empirical Study of Horizon Length

- Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.02572
- OpenReview: https://openreview.net/forum?id=PnHfrCMKtp
- Domain: LLM reasoning — agentic planning, long-horizon reinforcement learning, and credit assignment
- Status: Accepted to ICML 2026
- Posted: 2026-05-04

### Recommendation summary

This is the reasoning paper most worth reading today because it isolates a variable that agent papers often leave confounded: the number of dependent actions required to finish a task. Its central result is both scientifically useful and operationally actionable: even when the underlying reasoning rules are held roughly constant, longer horizons can destabilize RL through exploration failure and noisy credit assignment, while reducing the effective horizon with flexible macro-actions or verifiable subgoals restores learning. The paper is stronger as a controlled diagnosis and design principle than as a claim that it has solved realistic long-horizon agency.

### Why it matters now

Long-horizon agents are moving from benchmark curiosities toward coding, browsing, and research workflows, but many apparent “reasoning failures” may actually be failures of action granularity, reward structure, or trajectory length. This paper gives the field a cleaner experimental vocabulary—goal distance, interaction budget, and effective horizon—and shows why action-space and task-interface design should be treated as part of the learning problem. It is also a useful counterweight to another round of optimizer-centric or test-time-compute papers: the important intervention is structural, not merely more inference or a new policy-gradient variant.

### Core idea

- Construct Sudoku and Rush Hour tasks whose reasoning rules stay comparable while the minimum action sequence grows, isolating horizon length from intrinsic problem difficulty.
- Show that long-horizon RL becomes unstable because successful exploration becomes rarer and terminal feedback wrongly penalizes useful intermediate actions.
- Reduce the effective horizon with flexible macro-actions or verifiable subgoal decomposition, improving training stability and generalization to longer unseen trajectories.

### Broader trend

This belongs to the shift from single-turn chain-of-thought toward agentic reasoning as sequential decision-making. The durable thread is not simply “make agents reason longer,” but redesign actions, intermediate verification, curricula, and rewards so that long workflows remain learnable and diagnosable.

### What to read skeptically

The cleanest evidence comes from Sudoku and Rush Hour, with WebShop serving as a narrower realism check; real software, browser, or scientific agents add noisy observations, stochasticity, memory management, and changing environments. Most training experiments use small Qwen models (primarily 1.7B, with a 4B robustness check), so frontier-scale and cross-family generality remain open. Also keep “horizon generalization” distinct from reasoning-skill generalization: the paper itself finds that transfer breaks when Sudoku requires unseen, harder techniques, meaning the method extends familiar reasoning over more steps rather than reliably creating new reasoning primitives.
